National Day of Mourning: Indigenous Perspectives on Thanksgiving

Learn about the National Day of Mourning in Plymouth, its inception in 1970, and why Thanksgiving is a day of remembrance and activism for many Indigenous people.

Read the guideAll 20 articles
🍁 ThanksgivingHistory, Heritage & Perspectives

A different meaning on the same day

For over 50 years, the United American Indians of New England (UAINE) have been hosting a National Day of Mourning in Plymouth, Massachusetts on U.S. Thanksgiving. UAINE views this day as a time to pay tribute to Indigenous ancestors and their strength, reflect on the impact of colonization, and stand against ongoing racism and oppression.

This tradition coexists with, rather than merely mirrors, the family-oriented Thanksgiving observed by numerous Americans, showcasing how a single holiday can hold divergent historical significance.

How the observance began

UAINE links the inaugural National Day of Mourning to Wamsutta Frank James, an Aquinnah Wampanoag chief invited to address a 1970 event commemorating the Pilgrims' landing. Planners disapproved of his intended remarks, which questioned traditional narratives of colonial history.

The protest evolved into a yearly Indigenous-hosted event at Cole’s Hill in Plymouth.

Why language and attribution matter

When discussing the National Day of Mourning, it is crucial to credit the opinions of individuals and groups sharing them. UAINE views Thanksgiving as a stark reminder of genocide, appropriation of land, and erasure of culture for numerous Native individuals. This is their declared standpoint and the reason for their observance.

Don't generalize all Native nations as having the same viewpoint. Indigenous communities have a range of perspectives on Thanksgiving.

What respectful learning can look like

Families and schools can combine gratitude practices with learning accurate local history by recognizing whose homelands they reside on, engaging with Native-authored material, supporting modern Native organizations, and refraining from using stereotypical "Pilgrim and Indian" costumes. The aim is to promote historical awareness, not guilt or romanticization.

Frequently asked questions

When did the National Day of Mourning begin?

UAINE has organized the observance in Plymouth since 1970.

Do all Native Americans view Thanksgiving in the same way?

Indigenous nations and individuals are varied in their beliefs and traditions. The National Day of Mourning reflects a particular Indigenous-led custom and range of viewpoints, rather than a singular Native perspective.
