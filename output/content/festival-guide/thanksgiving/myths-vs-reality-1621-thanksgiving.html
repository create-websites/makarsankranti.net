Thanksgiving 1621: Myths, Reality and Wampanoag Perspectives

Distinguish the traditional Thanksgiving tale from the 1621 harvest event, Wampanoag negotiations, and Indigenous viewpoints that challenge the narrative.

Read the guideAll 20 articles
🍁 ThanksgivingHistory, Heritage & Perspectives

What the surviving record actually describes

The "first Thanksgiving" was a three-day harvest celebration at Plymouth in 1621, with English colonists, Ousamequin (Massasoit), and around ninety Wampanoag men present. The surviving English account briefly mentions fowling, recreational activities, and the Wampanoag gifting five deer, without depicting the neat school-play image later portrayed in popular culture.

Referring to the gathering as the first Thanksgiving may overshadow ancient traditions. Wampanoag communities had longstanding ceremonies of gratitude tied to harvests and nature, while English communities had days of thanksgiving and prayer pre-1621. The modern U.S. holiday emerged much afterwards.

Alliance, survival and political context

The meal took place as part of a political alliance, not in isolation. Ousamequin had formed a pact with the Plymouth colonists for mutual protection. Wampanoag support, which included farming expertise and diplomatic skills, was crucial for the colony's survival, while the alliance also benefited Wampanoag strategic goals in a shifting local environment.

The brief collaboration should not overshadow the enduring impacts of English settlement, land displacement, conflict, and upheaval for Indigenous peoples. An inclusive Thanksgiving narrative can recognize both the initial interaction in 1621 and the lasting effects of colonialism.

How the national story was built

The 1621 gathering did not become an annual national holiday right away. It was during the 19th century that writers and editors began to promote it as the founding story of the United States, and Thanksgiving proclamations and family traditions slowly shaped the holiday into its modern form.

In today's families and classrooms, the best approach is to avoid swapping one oversimplified narrative for another. Instead, we should differentiate between evidence, later myths, and current Indigenous viewpoints, acknowledging that Native nations are still thriving, varied, and modern.

A better way to talk about Thanksgiving

When discussing the events of 1621, it is important to use specific names like Wampanoag instead of generic labels. It is also crucial to avoid reducing living cultures to stereotypes through costumes and classroom activities. It is essential to explain who was present, what the sources actually say, and why the event holds different meanings for various communities.

Frequently asked questions

Was the 1621 gathering really the first Thanksgiving?

It could be more accurately characterized as a 1621 harvest celebration, drawing from both Native American and European traditions of gratitude, with the modern American Thanksgiving holiday emerging much later.

Were the Wampanoag simply invited dinner guests?

The political and strategic ties between Ousamequin and the Plymouth colonists were also intertwined with social connections, as evidenced by Wampanoag members bringing deer to the meeting.
