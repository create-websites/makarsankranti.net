What is the I Ching?

The I Ching—also written Yijing (易經), the Book of Changes—is an ancient Chinese classic with intertwined divinatory, philosophical and commentarial traditions.

易

A book of change

Its recurring concern is how to respond to shifting circumstances with awareness, humility and appropriate action.

☷

Line by line

Six stacked yin or yang lines form each hexagram. Traditional texts attach names, judgments and line statements to these figures.

鑑

A mirror for reflection

Consultation can be approached as a structured pause for examining a situation and considering possible responses—not fixed prophecy.

Two line types

Yin and yang build every figure

These line forms are relational rather than isolated opposites. Each gains meaning through position, combination and change.

Yang · unbroken

Traditionally associated with activity, firmness, expansion and initiating movement.

Yin · broken

Traditionally associated with receptivity, yielding, inwardness and responsive movement.

Reflective practice

A simple coin-method overview

The supplied slides present the three-coin method commonly used today. Detailed line values vary by convention, so use one trusted edition consistently.

Ask a clear question

Frame one genuine, specific situation. Open questions about perspective or action are more useful than demands for certainty.

Cast six lines

Toss three coins six times and record each line from the bottom of the hexagram upward.

Note changing lines

Depending on the coin values, certain results mark an old yin or old yang line that changes into its opposite.

Read the text

Consult the primary hexagram, relevant line statements and, when changing lines are present, the resulting hexagram.

Use with cultural respect and perspective The I Ching has a long history as a divinatory and philosophical text. Treat a consultation as symbolic reflection rather than scientifically validated prediction, and do not use it as a substitute for medical, legal, financial or other professional advice.
